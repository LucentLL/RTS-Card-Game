Spawn Row Duel Design Space

Every mechanic of consequence in Magic: The Gathering, Hearthstone and Yu-Gi-Oh! Master Duel, sorted by what it does rather than what it is called — so the same idea under three names collapses into one row, and what each game genuinely owns stands out. Then your game as the fourth column, the space none of them occupy, and — at the word level — the keyword lexicon: what gets printed on a card, which names are synonyms, and which words are still free.

The short version

Most of a card game is the same game. Enter-the-battlefield is Battlecry is “when Summoned”; Deathrattle is “when this dies”; Energy is Corpses is Xyz materials is your Vault; Secrets are Traps; Set is Morph is your charge. Roughly two-thirds of the functions below exist in all three games under different names. That is not a criticism — it is the vocabulary.

Each game owns three or four things. Magic: the stack, lands, blocking, attackable non-creatures. Hearthstone: the hero power, randomness, Secrets, Locations. Yu-Gi-Oh: no mana at all, the Extra Deck, five summon mechanics, Link arrows, the Chain. Everything else they share.

Your open space is the two things none of them have — a grid and buildings — plus the economy that runs on both. The closest any of the three comes is a Hearthstone Location (a permanent with durability and a once-a-turn activation) and a Yu-Gi-Oh Link arrow (a zone that points at another zone). Everything positional, everything about what a structure can do beyond print mana, and everything about workers as a resource you assign, is yours to define.

At the word level the same shape holds. Of 63 printed keywords, roughly forty are one idea wearing three names — Trample is Overwhelm is piercing; Battlecry is Play: is Opening Gambit. The unclaimed words cluster in exactly two places: position (nothing in the big three needs a word for “hits the next row”, because nothing has rows) and siegecraft (nothing needs a word for “extra damage to buildings”, because nothing has buildings). The military vocabulary — enfilade, sortie, cover, garrison, levy — is sitting there untouched.

What each game actually owns

The pillars nobody else copied

Magic

  • The stack and instant speed. Anyone can act at any time; responses nest. The richest interaction in the genre, and the slowest.
  • Lands. The resource is in the deck, so it varies — screw and flood are the game’s drama, and the colour pie hangs off it.
  • Blocking as the defender’s decision. The only one of the three where defence is played, not just declared.
  • Attackable non-creatures. Planeswalkers, then Battles: permanents the opponent protects.
  • The graveyard as a resource — flashback, delve, escape. Cards that come back are a design axis.
  • Time mechanics — suspend, Sagas, foretell, plot. Paying now for later.
  • Speed (2025): a global counter that ramps to Max — the nearest thing a CCG has to a tech level.

What it buys: depth and expressiveness. What it costs: a rules engine the size of a legal code, and twenty-minute games.

Hearthstone

  • The hero power. A small action you always have — the direct ancestor of your build menu, and Imbue lets it tech up.
  • Randomness on purpose. Discover, “a random Dragon”, generated cards. Only possible because it is digital; Magic forbids it.
  • Secrets — the entire reactive layer is hidden triggers, because nothing else happens on your opponent’s turn.
  • Locations (2022): a permanent with durability that you activate once a turn. The closest thing in any of the three to a structure that does something.
  • Weapons and armour — the hero fights and wears things.
  • The ten-crystal cap and no floating. Every late turn is still a decision.
  • Starships (2025): assemble a vehicle from parts over turns — a build mechanic, in a card game.

What it buys: pace and legibility — the benchmark for both. What it costs: interaction; the opponent’s turn is a cutscene.

Yu-Gi-Oh

  • No mana. The one Normal Summon a turn is the budget; the game is about breaking that limit. Turn one can be the whole game.
  • The Extra Deck — fifteen cards you always have, reached by converting your board: Fusion, Synchro, Xyz, Link, Pendulum. Five different summons, five different economies.
  • Link arrows. Zones that point at other zones. The only positional mechanic in any of the three — and it is only about where you may summon.
  • Pendulum scales — two “structures” you set to enable mass summoning every turn.
  • The Chain, negation, hand traps. Interaction is about stopping the other player’s engine, from anywhere, including your hand.
  • Set and Flip; attack / defence position. Face-down is a state, not a trick.
  • Hard once-per-turn as the universal safety valve.

What it buys: explosive turns and a toolbox always in reach. What it costs: readability — famously — and games decided before the second player acts.

The pattern: your cards are borrowed vocabulary — which is right; that is what makes them learnable. Your systems are original: the built economy, workers, the grid, structures on a tree, the settlement, raiding. The playtest said the same thing from the other side: the creatures are basic and the middle of the game is new. So the space to spend design on is where the original systems meet the cards — keywords and buildings that only make sense on a grid with an economy.

The open space

29 openings, by what only you can do

Tiered by how much they lean on the two things nobody else has. best fit uses the grid or the buildings and the economy at once; good fit uses one; rare is fine at the edges but should stay under your 10% for alternates; skip is the others’ identity, not yours.

Structures that do things

Activated structures — pay ◆ once a turn for an effect

best fit

A Barracks that spends ◆2 to raise a 1/1 militia; a Library that spends ◆1 to draw; a Watchtower that spends ◆1 to look at their top card. This is the Hearthstone Location, made a building. It also solves the problem the playtest measured hardest: 17 mana a turn with nothing to spend it on. Activated structures are mana sinks that scale with the economy they sit in.

precedent: Hearthstone Locations, hero powers · cost: one new StructEffect kind + a HUD button on the standee

Walls that actually block

best fit

Make eff:'wall' real: an attack that crosses a Bulwark’s row must be aimed at the Bulwark first, and a creature standing behind it in the same column cannot be attacked at all. Taunt for buildings. The dead effect becomes the reason sieges exist — and the reason the sapper has to bring a bigger army.

precedent: Taunt, Lure, Battles · cost: one clause in CombatEligibility · the playtest already found structure HP is the sapper lever; this is the same lever from the defender’s side

Production — a building that makes a unit every N turns

best fit

The RTS idiom in one card: a Barracks that raises a 2/2 Militia every second upkeep, crewed like a tower (⚒−1). Structures become the source of board presence, not just mana, and razing one is now removing a spawner — the sapper’s reason to exist without touching the wall rule.

precedent: Assemble the Legion (Magic), Starship pieces (Hearthstone) · cost: a StructEffect with a counter, like Chrysalis

Garrison — a creature inside a building

best fit

Move a creature onto a friendly structure’s cell: it cannot be attacked or targeted, the structure gains its attack and retaliates with it, and destroying the structure ejects it damaged. A tower becomes a tower with an archer in it. Nothing in the three games has this — Vehicles and Crew are the nearest and they are a shadow of it.

precedent: Vehicles + Crew (Magic) · cost: a cell can hold two objects; medium

Structures as gates

best fit

Two flavours, both proposed already: forge-gated colour spells (“needs a Gloomwell”) and tier-gated creatures (“needs a Keep”). This is how RTS games make buildings matter without giving them abilities: no Templar Archives, no High Templar. It is also a mana sink in disguise — you build the gate to unlock the card. Magic’s Speed counter (2025) is the only CCG analogue of a tech level, and it is global; yours is on the board and can be razed.

precedent: StarCraft tech tree; Speed (Aetherdrift) · cost: a needs field on cards + one check in PlayCardHandler

Structure adjacency

good fit

A Forge beside the Base yields one more; a Barracks gives the creature in front of it +50; two Encampments side by side share a crew. City-builder logic on your grid. Only a game with buildings and positions can have it.

precedent: none in card games; SimCity, Anno · cost: adjacency lookup in the upkeep tick

Under construction

good fit

Structures take a turn to complete, and a worker standing beside one finishes it now. The tower already works this way in effect (built this turn, fires next harvest); make it universal, visible, and something workers can spend themselves on. Raiding a half-built forge is a real play.

precedent: Suspend, Plot · cost: a flag + a turn counter on StructureUnit

Reactive structures

good fit

A tower that fires when a creature enters its row; a Bulwark that raises a shield when its row is attacked; a Ward Tower that negates the first enemy spell each turn. Yu-Gi-Oh’s traps are cards; yours can be buildings — permanent, visible, razeable interaction, bounded by once-per-turn. It answers the “the opponent’s turn is a cutscene” problem Hearthstone has without importing the stack.

precedent: Secrets, Trap monsters, Counter Traps · cost: structures as trigger sources in Triggers.cs

Repair

good fit

Nothing heals a structure today. A worker action — ◆1 and a tapped worker to restore 100 — makes damaged buildings a decision instead of a countdown, and gives workers a third thing to do.

precedent: none (Magic proliferate, loosely) · cost: one command

Siege objectives on their side

rare

Magic’s Battles: a permanent you put on the opponent’s side that they must defend. A Siege Camp planted in their front row that wins a reward if it stands for three turns. Keep it a reward, not a win, and it is a great use of the grid.

precedent: Battles / Sieges (Magic 2023) · keep under the 10%

A wonder

rare

A late, expensive structure that wins if it survives N turns. Every one of the three has one famous alternate win; this would be yours, and it is a structure, which is right. Rare by cost.

precedent: Exodia, Quests, Azor’s Elocutors · keep under the 10%

Position — the thing only a grid has

Ranged creatures, and Reach

best fit

A creature that attacks across rows without moving — blockable by the row-interval rule, exactly like the tower shot you want blockable. Then Reach as the answer: a creature that may block ranged attacks from any row. This turns your existing blocking geometry into a rock-paper-scissors of range, and gives the tower a family instead of a monopoly.

precedent: Reach (Magic, inverted) · cost: attack declaration without movement; the resolver already handles crossed rows

Adjacency auras

best fit

“Creatures beside this get +50.” Link arrows are Yu-Gi-Oh’s one positional idea, and they only decide where you may summon; an aura that reads the neighbouring cells is the same idea with teeth. Formation becomes a decision every turn.

precedent: Link arrows; Duelyst’s adjacency · cost: cheap — a keyword that reads the four neighbours

Movement keywords

good fit

Charge: move and attack in one turn. Slow: move or attack, not both. Swift: two moves. Phalanx: moves as a group. Haste and Rush are the only movement-adjacent keywords the others have, and they are about summoning sickness. Yours would be about the board.

precedent: Haste / Rush · cost: flags on MoveUnit

Terrain rules on the middle row

good fit

The arena already rolls a biome. Let it mean something: forest gives the centre row stealth (cannot be attacked from two rows away), sand slows movement across it, stone gives structures there +100. A global rule that changes every match, drawn from what is already on screen.

precedent: Field Spells (Yu-Gi-Oh), Duelyst tiles · cost: a per-biome rule table

Zone control

good fit

A structure that makes the row in front of it cost ◆1 to enter, or that stops enemy creatures ending a move beside it. Bunkers. The grid finally has choke points, and the centre row is worth fighting over for a reason beyond being in the way.

precedent: none · cost: a movement-cost hook

Cover

good fit

A creature with a friendly structure in front of it (same column, nearer the enemy) cannot be hit by towers or ranged attacks. Buildings become shelter as well as economy; where you put the Forge now matters to the army.

precedent: none · cost: a column check in FireTower / ranged targeting

Flank and rear

good fit

Attacking from the centre row into their front gives +50 (flank); a creature with a friendly creature in front of it in the same column cannot be attacked (rear guard). Formation as a mechanic, not just a picture.

precedent: Summoner Wars, Duelyst · cost: two eligibility clauses

Workers and the economy

Contested income on the middle row

best fit

Magic’s Monarch and Initiative are contested statuses — whoever holds them draws. Yours can be a place: a Mine that stands on the centre row and yields ◆2 to whoever controls the cells around it. King of the hill, on a board that already has a hill. The centre row stops being a corridor.

precedent: the Monarch, the Initiative · cost: a neutral structure + a control check

Hiring — mana into workers

best fit

Pay ◆2 at a structure to add a worker to its row, up to what the row’s buildings can house. Today support is fixed by the building; with hiring it is a choice, mana has somewhere to go, and “workers are resources” is literally true — you buy them.

precedent: Corpses, inverted · cost: a command + a housing cap

Worker assignment

good fit

Each turn a worker either harvests, or builds (finishes construction), or garrisons (adds +50 to the structure it stands in). The RTS economy proper. The deepest version of your unique layer, and the one with the most UI.

precedent: StarCraft, Agricola · cost: high — a per-worker state and a way to set it on a phone

Supply as a printed keyword

good fit

Creatures already carry upkeep. Print it as a keyword family: Quartermaster (other creatures in this row cost one less to keep), Elite (costs two, hits harder), Levy (free to keep, dies at end of turn). The settlement phase becomes a place cards are designed for, not just a tax.

precedent: StarCraft supply · cost: keyword hooks in WorkerMath

Spend the vault

good fit

Magic’s Energy pays for abilities. Let banked ◆ be an alternate cost — a spell that costs “◆3, or ◆1 from the vault” — and the Vault becomes a strategy instead of a buffer.

precedent: Energy · cost: a second cost field

Cards, colour, and the rest

Tribes that do something

good fit

Dragon, Warrior, Wizard, Human are on the cards and nothing reads them. The cheapest depth available: “+50 for each other Dragon you control”, “Wizards cost one less to keep”. Yu-Gi-Oh’s entire archetype system is this.

precedent: tribal (Magic), minion types (Hearthstone), Types (Yu-Gi-Oh) · cost: trivial

Devotion scaling for premium colour spells

good fit

“Deal 50, +25 for each Dark creature you control.” Never a dead card, rewards commitment. Pair with the forge gate as the floor; use your “needs a Dark creature” for signature cards only.

precedent: Devotion (Theros), Spell Damage · cost: a count in SpellEngine

A second action phase

good fit

Magic and Yu-Gi-Oh both act again after combat; you do not. Building after the attack — rebuilding what they razed, raising a tower where the line held — is the RTS rhythm, and it is one phase-machine edge.

precedent: Main Phase 2 · cost: one transition

Search

good fit

Eight creatures an element is a thin pool; Yu-Gi-Oh makes thin pools consistent with search. A structure or spell that tutors a creature of your element from the deck fixes consistency without adding cards.

precedent: search (Yu-Gi-Oh), tutors · cost: trivial

Digital-native choice

good fit

You are digital. A Recruit structure that offers a choice of three creatures from your pool, a rolled terrain that changes the rules, a hidden worker count. Hearthstone owns randomness in the genre; you can take the choice half of it without the coin flips.

precedent: Discover · cost: a choice request, which the engine already has

Siege units

good fit

“Siege: double damage to structures.” “Breach: may attack the wall past a structure.” Trample for a game with buildings. Makes the sapper a deck you build rather than a plan you hope for.

precedent: Trample, Piercing · cost: two damage modifiers

The rest of the evergreen set

good fit

Lifelink, Deathtouch, Enrage/Frenzy, Freeze, Windfury, Taunt, Choose One, board wipes, silence, counterspell. All missing, all common vocabulary, all cheap. Not where the identity is — but the creatures are basic because these are absent, and every one is a few lines in KeywordEngine.

precedent: everywhere · cost: low, one at a time

Skip: the stack, hand traps, hand-position mechanics

skip

Nested responses are Magic’s identity and its cost; instant-speed interaction from hand is Yu-Gi-Oh’s. Outcast (Hearthstone) reads the position of a card in your hand, which has no meaning on a board. Your reactive layer should be buildings, above.

The keyword lexicon

What is actually printed on the card

The word layer rather than the system layer. Two extra columns earn their place: Yu-Gi-Oh has almost no formal keywords — it writes full sentences, and the community supplies the vocabulary (piercing, floodgate, hand trap, omni-negate, hard once-per-turn), so that column carries community terms in italics. And the grid CCGs — Duelyst, Legends of Runeterra — matter more to you than to anyone, because Duelyst is the closest thing to your board that has ever shipped and it had to name all of this already.

Read across: where four columns are filled, the idea is settled vocabulary and you should borrow the clearest name rather than invent. Where the row is mostly empty, the games did not need a word — and those gaps are the next section.

Three things to take from the table. First, where four columns are filled, use the clearest existing word — a player who knows any of these games gets your card for free, and inventing a synonym costs you that. Second, Yu-Gi-Oh proves the opposite case: refuse to name a thing and the community names it for you (piercing, floodgate, brick, garnet, omni-negate). Naming your own mechanics is free identity, and it is the cheapest identity there is. Third, the position and structure blocks are nearly empty, and the only column that fills them at all is Duelyst — a grid game that shipped in 2016 and had to invent Blast, Zeal, Backstab, Airdrop and Provoke because nothing existed to borrow.

Words that are still free

Keyword-level open space

Names not spoken for by Magic, Hearthstone, Yu-Gi-Oh, Runeterra or Duelyst, that mean something obvious on a board with rows, buildings and an upkeep. Each is a keyword you could print tomorrow. best fit needs the grid or the structures to make sense at all — those are the ones no competitor can copy without building your board first.

Siegecraft — the empty category

Siege N

best fit

Deals N extra damage to structures. The single most obviously missing word in your game: buildings are attackable, they have big health bars, and no card is for killing them. Caution: Magic uses “Siege” as a Battle subtype, so it is not virgin ground — but as a creature keyword it reads clean, and no one prints it on a body.

alternatives if you want it fully clear: Breaker, Demolish, Sapper

Breach

best fit

May attack the wall past an enemy structure in its column — or, when it razes a structure, may attack again. Trample for a game with buildings, and the reason a siege deck converts instead of stalling. Unused anywhere as a keyword.

nearest: Trample, Overwhelm — neither is about buildings

Salvage N

best fit

When a structure you control is destroyed, gain ◆N. Turns losing a forge from a spiral into a swing, and gives the defender of a siege something to play for. “Scavenge” is taken by Magic; Salvage is free and more precise.

nearest: Magic’s Scavenge (taken, and unrelated)

Emplacement

good fit

This creature may not move, but hits at range. A stationary gun as a creature rather than a building — the bridge between your two card types. Completely unclaimed, and unmistakably military.

nearest: Defender + Ranged, which no single game has together

Position

Enfilade

best fit

Hits every enemy in the target’s row. The military word for firing along a line, unused by any card game, and it describes your board exactly. Duelyst called its version Blast; enfilade is better and free.

nearest: Blast (Duelyst)

Volley

best fit

Attacks a cell two rows away without moving, and cannot attack adjacent. Artillery: real range with a real weakness. Pairs with Reach (borrow Magic’s word, invert its meaning: may block ranged attacks) to make range answerable.

nearest: Ranged (Duelyst) — simpler; Volley adds the minimum range that makes it interesting

Cover

best fit

Cannot be hit by ranged attacks or towers while a friendly structure stands in front of it. Makes where you put the Forge a decision the army cares about. The word is universal in shooters and strategy games and untouched in card games.

nearest: nothing — needs both a grid and buildings

Flank

good fit

+N when attacking from the centre row into their front. Magic had “Flanking” in 1997 (a blocker penalty, long retired), so it is nearly free and the meaning is intuitive.

nearest: Backstab (Duelyst); Flanking (Magic, retired)

Vanguard

good fit

This creature must be attacked before others in its row — Taunt, but scoped to a row instead of a whole board, which is what a grid lets you say. Also a natural name for “the front line” in your fiction.

nearest: Taunt, Provoke — neither is positional

Phalanx

good fit

+N while a friendly creature stands beside it. Adjacency as an identity rather than a one-off. Free, and it says “formation” without a rules paragraph.

nearest: Zeal (Duelyst), Support (LoR)

Advance / Skirmish

good fit

Advance: may move and attack in the same turn. Skirmish: may move again after attacking. Haste and Rush are about summoning sickness; these are about the board, and neither word is taken.

nearest: Celerity (Duelyst)

Supply — keywords for an economy nobody else has

Levy

best fit

Costs no upkeep, but returns to your hand (or dies) at end of turn. A conscript: free to field, impossible to hold. Your upkeep system makes this a real trade-off; in any other game it is just a bad card.

nearest: Ephemeral (LoR) — but the reason is yours

Quartermaster

best fit

Other creatures in this row cost one less ⚒ to keep. A support unit whose whole job is the settlement phase — a card designed for a system only you have.

nearest: nothing

Forage / Requisition

best fit

“Forage” is taken by Magic (2024). Requisition N is free: tap a worker to gain ◆N or a benefit — a card that spends the workforce directly, which is the resource nothing else has.

nearest: Convoke (Magic) — tapping bodies, but for casting cost only

Conscript

good fit

Turn one of your workers into a 1/1 creature (or this costs one less for each worker you sacrifice). The clearest possible statement that workers are a resource you spend, not units you keep.

nearest: Tribute (Yu-Gi-Oh), sacrifice costs

Garrison

good fit

May enter a friendly structure: untargetable while inside, the structure gains its attack. Magic shipped Station in 2025 for almost exactly this (crew a Spacecraft by putting creatures on it), which is proof the idea works — and Garrison is the better word for a castle.

nearest: Station, Crew, Saddle (Magic)

Borrow these outright — do not invent a synonym

Trample, Deathtouch, Lifelink, Taunt, Freeze, Vigilance, Reach

borrow

Settled in three or four games each. Every one is missing from your card set, every one is a few lines in KeywordEngine, and every one is understood on sight by a player from any of the three. Taunt in particular: you have Provoke on a trap, but no body that demands to be dealt with, which is the most basic defensive card in the genre. Reach is the answer to your own towers and to Scour.

This is the cheapest fix for “the creatures are very basic” — not new words, the standard ones you skipped

Do not use: Ward, Forge, Charge, Rush, Station, Crew, Exhaust, Scavenge, Forage, Reinforce, Plot, Craft, Discover, Echo, Combo, Titan

taken

All are live keywords in Magic or Hearthstone with settled meanings. Two are already in your game pointing the wrong way: Ward (yours summons a blocker; Magic’s taxes targeting) and Forge (your central building; Hearthstone’s keyword for “pay 2 to improve a card” — a mild collision, and arguably fine since yours is a card name, not a keyword).

Name collisions to fix before players notice

Same word, different meaning

Ward

In Magic, Ward is a tax on targeting (“Ward 2”). Yours conjures a token blocker. A Magic player will read your card wrong every time. Escort or Aegis says what it does.

Overcharge

Sits one letter from Hearthstone’s Overload, which is the opposite (a debt). Yours banks and discharges — Capacitor or Charge, except Charge is Hearthstone’s haste. Store is plain and free.

Chrysalis

Fine — distinct from Dormant, and a better word for what it does. Keep it.

First Strike, Detonate, Reap

All clear. First Strike is Magic’s and means the same; Detonate and Reap are yours and read at a glance.

Scour

Not a keyword anywhere else; a Magic card name only. Safe.

Forge

Hearthstone made Forge a keyword in 2023 — pay 2 to improve a card. Yours is a building name, not a keyword, so the two rarely meet on a page; worth knowing before you ever print “Forge” as an ability.

Base, Keep, Vault

Plain English, no collisions. The Vault will be compared to Energy — that is a compliment.

Games taken as they stand in early 2026: Magic through Edge of Eternities and Aetherdrift (Speed, Exhaust, Station), Hearthstone through Imbue and Starships, Yu-Gi-Oh as Master Duel plays it. Duelyst and Legends of Runeterra are included in the keyword lexicon because they are the only shipped CCGs that had to name positional mechanics. New keywords arrive quarterly; the functions do not.
