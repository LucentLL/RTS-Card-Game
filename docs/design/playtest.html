Spawn Row Duel Playtest

Six hundred and forty headless matches after the economy pass, through two lenses: eight scripted playstyles piloting the game like a person would, and the shipped C# rules played against themselves. What wins, what stalls, and how it stands against Hearthstone, MTG Arena and Master Duel.

320 pilot matches · 8 playstyles × 12 decks 320 self-play matches · 8×8 commanders × 5 seeds 16 A/B variants × 96 320 more self-play, with deck-out 0 crashes or invariant failures

The verdict in three lines

The game has a genuinely new middle — a build-order economy and a positional board that none of the three comparison games have — and a first-player split of 49/51, which most of them would envy.

It had a broken late game. An unblockable ranged building and an infinite free revive made a treadmill that 14% of self-play matches never left, because nothing ended a game but the wall. Deck-out is a loss as of this evening: every stall now ends — and 40% of AI-vs-AI games end that way, the first player losing all of them, which is the treadmill still standing underneath. In human play deck-out never fires.

Rushing the wall is the dominant human strategy at 68–73%, winning in thirteen plies. The single biggest lever the A/B found was the one you named: halve structure HP and a siege pilot goes from 17% to 83% while the rush does not move. A hard mana cap made things worse. Going second still costs a pilot half its wins.

Two lenses

Method

Pilots vs the AI · the strategy lens

tools/playtest/ boots the living JS game in jsdom and plays it through a human’s entry points: eight playstyles (aggro, wallrush, multiattack, balanced, turtle, hunter, sapper, raider) with matching deck shapes, against the shipped scripted AI. This is the richer read on what a person would do. Caveat: the JS is the ruleset the port left behind — three centre lanes, no row gate on builds, no sacrifice gate — so it describes a close cousin of the game on Pages, on identical card data.

320 matches · 8 playstyles × 8 mono + 4 dual decks × 3 seeds · 160-ply cap

Self-play · the shipped-rules lens

PlaytestCli (new today) runs the C# engine the player actually gets — open middle row, row gates, sacrifice gate, this morning’s economy — every mono commander against every other, both sides driven by the scripted AI, draining engine events after each command. It cannot show human strategy, but it is the only honest source for game length, stalls, and where the mana comes from.

The sapper’s 0% turned out to be the pilot, not the rules: every one of its matches ended with the enemy at 10000 life, because it never attacks the wall at all. The siege pilot — raze while they still have an economy, then convert — is what the question needed, and it is 17% under the shipped numbers. What moves it is in the levers table below. The raider persona is retired: workers are a resource in the design, not units to kill.

The economy pass made rushing better, not worse: the Base’s ⚒+1 slowed everyone’s early workers, which raised the relative value of a cheap creature on turn two. 57% of all declared attacks deal no wall damage and blocks are taken 83% of the time they’re offered — and the wall still falls in six rounds, because a joint attack averages 1432 against a single attack’s 815 and only the attacker chooses the row.

Emergent strategies

Ranked by what they win

The rush

68–73% · 13 plies

Base, forge, a third building, then cheap bodies at the wall every turn. The economy never matters because the game is over before it exists. Joint attacks are the multiplier; blocks slow it, retaliation taxes it, nothing stops it. This is the human meta.

pilots: aggro 73%, wallrush 68%, multiattack 58% · wins median 13 plies · blocks 83%, zero-damage attacks 57%

The tower–reliquary treadmill

degenerate · 14% never end

Cannon Towers shoot the first enemy creature every upkeep, unblockably. The Reliquary hands back the most recent dead creature every upkeep, for free, forever. Summon → shot → returned → summon. Games with above-median tower fire run twice as long (79 turns vs 40). All 45 stalls have a Reliquary standing, average four towers on the board, 140 revives per side, both decks empty and both walls above 70% — and there is no deck-out rule to end it.

self-play: 157 tower shots/match, 40 revives/side; stalls 45/45 with reliquary, 4.0 towers, 139.6 revives/side, deck 0

Mana flood

from turn ~10

Both sides settle at 16–18 mana a turn and cannot spend it: 80 mana drained per side in short games, 447 in long ones. The Vault’s ◈4/◈10 is noise against that. Once mana is unlimited the only scarce thing is cards, which is why the Reliquary — the sole card-advantage engine — is on every stalled board. The trade-off you set this morning holds at the low end (harvest 53% / structures 47%); it is the ceiling that’s missing.

self-play: 17.0 mana/side/turn; drained 79 (<40t) → 225 (40–70t) → 447 (70t+); winner hand 7.9

Glass cannons rush, tanks grind

element identity

Attack-per-mana predicts the pilot lens (Dark, Wind, Electric on top); the 1000-damage tower threshold predicts the grind (a 938-HP body dies to one shot, so Wind and Electric collapse to 20%). Dark sits on top of both because Reap turns each tower kill into a token. Earth’s 2125-HP walls win nothing anywhere: they can’t race and there’s nothing to outlast.

atk/mana: dark 729, wind/electric 646, earth 292 · self-play dark 79%, wind 20% · piloted electric 66%, earth 10%

Go first

2 : 1 in the pilot lens

A pilot that concedes the opening turn drops from 33% to 16%. In symmetric self-play the split is 49/51 — the scripted AI doesn’t press tempo, a person does. The coin-flip opener in ruleset v3 randomises who suffers this; it doesn’t compensate for it.

A/B go-second: 33% → 16% · self-play first-player 136 of 275

Siege: sap, then convert

17% now · 83% at half structure HP

Raze while the enemy still has an economy standing, then take the wall with the board you built while they rebuilt. Under the shipped numbers it wins 17%: a 2500–3000 HP building costs two creature-turns to fell, the AI rebuilds at 17 mana a turn, and every turn spent on a forge is a turn not spent on the wall. Halve structure HP and it becomes the best plan in the game — 83%, median 23 plies, enemy at 0 life — and the rush does not move, because the rush never attacks a building anyway. The blocker was HP, exactly as suspected.

A/B struct-half-hp: siege 17% → 83%, sapper 0% → 50%, balanced 58 → 75, hunter 50 → 75, turtle 8 → 50, aggro 75 → 75

Where it stands

Strong · weak

Strong · the economy is a game

Build order, worker placement, upkeep, which row a building stands in — a strategic layer with real decisions that no mainstream CCG offers. This morning’s trade-off makes the opening three buildings three different plans.

Weak · the economy has no ceiling

By turn ten it prints 17 a turn and drains hundreds. Nothing you do with mana matters after the opening. A hard cap is not the answer — it collapsed every slow plan in the A/B. Making buildings killable is: then the ceiling is your opponent.

Strong · the board is real

Rows, columns, movement, blocking by interval, retaliation, ranged fire. Position is a resource, and the 83% block rate says the defender is playing too.

Weak · the economy cannot be attacked

The wall is rightly the win; the problem is that the route through the enemy’s economy is closed. Buildings take two creature-turns to fell and are rebuilt in one. A siege wins 17%; at half HP, 83%.

Strong · consistency

Structures outside the deck mean every game has its engine; 40/3 keeps decks honest. No “didn’t draw it” games. Symmetric play splits 49/51 on turn order.

Weak · the tower

◆4 for 1000 unblockable damage every upkeep, forever, from a building that never has to attack. 157 shots a match. Doubles game length, defines the grind, and makes half the roster unplayable in it. Removing it makes things worse (the AI’s economy explodes); halving it helps.

Strong · it can be fast

A rush ends in six rounds. The game has a tempo mode that fits a phone.

Weak · it can be very slow

A grind is thirty rounds at nine commands each. That is an RTS turn, and the interface is a phone in landscape.

Strong · element identity

Eight keywords, eight stat profiles, balanced on total stats per mana. The elements play differently and the data shows it.

Weak · element balance and pool depth

20–79% by commander. Eight creatures an element means the element is the deck; there is no room for two Dark decks to differ.

What to change, in order

Each with its A/B number

Deck-out is a loss — shipped

Done this evening; every stall ends. It also revealed that in 40% of AI-vs-AI games nobody reaches the wall, and that the first player loses all of those because it draws first. Pair it with the standard first-player draw skip, which also answers the go-second problem below; or soften it to Hearthstone fatigue (escalating damage per empty draw) so the wall still decides. Your call — both are a few lines.

self-play: stalls 45 → 0 · deck-out decides 128/320 · first player 49% → 31% (wall games alone: 52%)

Cut structure HP — the biggest lever measured

Half made the siege the best plan in the game and lifted every non-rush pilot, without touching the rush. Try a third off first, or make the economy buildings (forges, encampments) fragile and the Base sturdy. This is also the mana ceiling: an economy that can be razed is an economy that is not unlimited.

A/B struct-half-hp: overall 31% → 54%, siege 17 → 83, sapper 0 → 50, aggro 75 → 75, median 24 → 20 plies

Cannon Tower at 50, Bombard tier at 100 — shipped

Done: the tower fires for 500 and crews one worker; a Bombard Tower (◆5, ♥300, ⚒−2) is the upgrade back to the full shot behind thinner walls. Measured in self-play: the AI upgrades 2.8 towers a side into Bombards and the grind barely moves (85 → 82 shots a match) — at 17 mana a turn ◆5 is not a decision. That is the case for the rule that would actually bite: tower fire that can be blocked, with towers auto-declaring in combat and the defender interposing by the row-interval rule. About a day in the resolver; on the table. For now, the user’s timing rule is in: towers fire at harvest, after Move/Pay/Sacrifice, and only from a row that can crew them — a tower must be built, survive an enemy turn, and be paid for before its first shot.

A/B towers-500: 33% → 42% · self-play after: shots 85 → 82, first player 31% → 45%, deck-out endings 40% → 36%

Do not hard-cap mana

A ceiling of 10 took the pilot from 31% to 22%: wallrush rose, and balanced, hunter and turtle collapsed to nothing. A cap punishes whoever was building. Let the flood be answered by sieges instead.

A/B mana-cap-10: balanced 58 → 8, hunter 50 → 0, turtle 8 → 0, wallrush 58 → 83

Compensate going second

The first-player draw skip is the one the comparison games use and it now does double duty with deck-out. A free Encampment on turn one is the RTS-flavoured alternative.

A/B go-second: 33% → 16%

Bring Wind and Electric up, Dark down

Reap is worth ~20 points in the grind. Give Wind and Electric a death value, or bodies over the tower line, and put Dark’s token on a cost.

self-play after deck-out: dark 80%, wind 23%, electric 26%
